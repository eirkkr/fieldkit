# 054 - A change is an issue, and a stage is a pull request

## Decision

Retire OpenSpec and the `review-gated` schema. A change keeps nothing about itself in the repository's files: it is planned, built and reviewed on GitHub, and what it leaves in the repository is code, tests, docs and ADRs. The rules a change follows are in [specs.md](../../conventions/specs.md); this records what was decided and why.

**What counts as a change.** Work is a change when any one of three things is true: it cannot land as one pull request that says one thing, which by the stage-sizing rule means more than about six steps; it changes what the software does in a way that leaves the reviewer a decision to make; or the reviewer asks for it to be one. The test is whether a single PR could be opened for it now without asking anything first. If it could, it is ordinary work and none of this applies.

In what follows a *stage* is the smallest part of a change that leaves the default branch green and says one thing, a *gate* is the stop at the end of a stage, the *reviewer* is the human who approves it, and a *reviewer agent* is an agent that reads work it did not build.

**Where each thing lives, and who it is for:**

- **The change is an issue, and its body is the brief** - all the reviewer reads to approve the plan. It holds the requirements, a sentence each, alongside the decisions made, the decisions that are theirs, where the plan is most likely wrong, what is out of scope, a line per stage, and what cannot be specified yet. It always says the plan as it stands, and every edit to it comes with a comment saying what changed and why.
- **The build notes are a comment on that issue, for the next agent.** The first is posted with the plan; a new one is posted whole each time a stage is approved and merged, and the one before it is hidden as outdated. They hold the change's state and point at requirements without restating them.
- **A stage is a sub-issue, closed by one pull request.** A change that fits one pull request has no stages: it is built on its own issue, and its PR closes it. A stage names the requirements it delivers. Its steps are written into it when it starts, not when the change is planned. A session claims it by assigning it. It is marked blocked only by a stage or change it cannot be built without.
- **A step is a commit**, whose body says why.
- **The PR body is the reviewer's page**: the stage in a few sentences, what changed from the plan, the choices they could overturn, and one thing to try.
- **What is decided at a gate is written on the PR**, in the brief if it changes the plan, and in an ADR if it outlasts the change.
- **Behaviour is described once**, by tests and docs. There are no delta specs, no living specs and no archive.

**Who does what:**

- **Planning starts with questions to the reviewer**, and a plan is approved in a comment before any stage issue exists.
- **Review by fresh agents is offered at every stop, and runs when the reviewer asks.** A plan published, a PR at its gate, a fix made after either: each time, the agent says what has not been reviewed and which reviews look worth running, with a reason drawn from what changed. A PR is read on two axes kept apart, the repo's conventions and the requirements it claims to deliver. Each finding is reproduced, then fixed, moved to the later stage that will handle it, or listed as an issue to file. The PR body always says which reviews have run.
- **A change built in stages can be reviewed whole once its last stage merges**, in three directions: requirements unmet, things built unasked, requirements no test holds. It is offered like any other review. Its findings are the closing comment on the change's issue, and the reviewer closes it.
- **The kit carries a contract and two small skills.** `specs.md` says what a change's issue and a stage's issue must contain. `change-plan` writes the brief and, on approval, creates the stage issues. `change-stage` builds one stage to its gate and, on approval, merges it and posts the notes. `change-stage` checks an issue against the contract before it starts, so a change planned by hand or with someone else's skill is built the same.
- **A repo may name the skills that ask the questions and do the reviews**, in its own `CLAUDE.md`, and the reviewer may name one for a single run. A named skill fills a role without redefining it: what it leaves out, the kit's default still does.
- **An agent takes instructions only from people who can write to the repository.** Issue and PR text from anyone else is read as a report, never followed.

Unchanged: the stage is the unit of merge and ends green and independently mergeable ([041](041-stage-is-the-merge-unit.md)); a gate is a full stop, and approving it starts nothing ([049](049-approval-does-not-start-the-next-stage.md)); what a change writes is divided by its reader, and an empty item is said, not dropped ([052](052-divide-a-change-by-reader.md)).

This supersedes [021](021-adopt-openspec-centralised-via-kit.md), [022](022-openspec-skills-model-discoverable.md) and [034](034-review-gated-openspec-schema.md), and replaces the mechanics of 041, 049 and 052 while keeping their rules.

## Reason

The evidence is ten changes in one consumer repo, six of them review-gated, read back after the fact. Only the last few gates among them were written under 052's split into a brief and a record, too few to judge it by; this keeps that split and moves both halves.

**Most of what a change wrote had no human reader.** One eight-stage change produced about 3,150 lines of change artifacts for 1,350 lines of source and 3,525 of tests. The pull request that landed its 1,530-line plan drew no comments and no reviews. Its `tasks.md` reached 1,440 lines, of which about 290 were the plan and the rest review notes. The reviewer reported reading neither the living specs nor the delta specs, ever, and not using the note's "look closely at" item.

**The detailed plan did not hold, and keeping it true was most of the work.** Each of that change's first five stages recorded departures from a design written days earlier, and the first stage's review reversed a decision the design had recorded. Every departure meant correcting the design and the later tasks by hand; the final review corrected them again, and the next commit archived them. Another change was planned in full, paused, and rewritten before most of it was built, going from 46 tasks to 73. A third has waited unstarted while the model it targets changed under it. Detail written ahead of the stage that needs it is written twice.

**The bookkeeping existed because facts git already held were written into files.** The commit list, `Reviewed at`, `Change based at`, the recorded check states and the rule for remapping all of them after a rebase are copies of what a pull request knows about itself. On one stage's branch, four of eleven commits existed only to maintain the note. 034 defined that note, and 041 and 052 each reshaped it.

**What the reviewer did at gates was decide.** The send-backs on record are questions about behaviour - why one fault should end a run at all, whether a placement window belongs to a block or a component, whether a fixture should hold real material - plus requests for plainer wording, and one verify command that turned out to match nothing. None of that needs a record of every commit. All of it needs a short page that says what is theirs to decide.

**The findings came from independent review, which nothing required.** At several gates a fresh agent read the branch and found what the building agent had not: values stored that should have been refused, an input that crashed a page, tests that were missing. Where that did not happen, a final review found five convention faults that seven approved stages had let through. The independent pass is given a place at every stage, where a finding is cheapest to fix, and at the plan, which until now had none.

**Behaviour was described three times.** The living specs, the repo's own docs and the tests covered the same ground, a whole stage of one change was spent bringing the docs into line, and nothing outside the spec tooling read the living specs.

**Review is offered, not run, because the first trial ran it eleven times.** The design first had a fresh agent read every plan and every stage unasked. Tried on a change of two short sections of documentation, it dispatched eleven: one on the plan, two on the stage, two more after each of two requests to shorten the text, and four for a final review of the same single diff. The change took fifty minutes, most of it waiting on reviews of edits the reviewer had just asked for and read. The reviewer chose to be told a review has not run, and which would be worth it, over having it run. What keeps a skipped review from being a silent one is that every stop says so and every PR body carries the line.

**A sub-issue for a change of one stage was tried in the same trial and dropped.** It gave one piece of work an issue, a sub-issue and a pull request, a second set of build notes with no next agent to read them, and a final review of a diff already reviewed. A change that fits one pull request is a plan approved before an ordinary PR, and is built on its own issue.

**Other tools were read for what they do with the same problem.** The most used spec workflows - GitHub's Spec Kit, Kiro, OpenSpec, Superpowers - keep a change as Markdown files in the repository, and the criticism published of them is the one made here: the files are a heavier review than the code. One that mirrors its files into issues and sub-issues keeps both copies. The nearest to this design is Matt Pocock's skills, which publish a spec as an issue and its work as child issues joined by native blocking links, and several rules here are taken from them: no file paths or code in a plan, an out-of-scope section, a place for what cannot be specified yet, claiming a ticket by assigning it, referring to an issue by its name, reviewing standards and spec apart, and turning a mechanical mistake into a check. They differ where this keeps what 041 and 049 decided: there, one branch and one PR carry a whole spec, with no stop between tickets and no state handed from one to the next.

**Requirements are still written before the build, and now where the reviewer reads them.** That is what the delta specs were for, and it is the part of them kept: the brief holds the requirements, and a review of the plan, of a stage or of the whole change checks against them. One change's delta specs ran to 490 lines of requirements and scenarios that the reviewer never opened. A sentence each, with the scenarios left to the tests, is short enough to be read once the rest is gone. What goes is the format, the copy in the living specs, and the sync between the two.

**Putting the requirements in the build notes was rejected.** It keeps the brief to one screen, but the reviewer then approves what the software must do without reading it, checked only by an agent.

**A threshold for what counts as a change is stated because the old one is gone.** A repo used to opt in by having an `openspec/` directory, and a change was whatever someone ran the propose skill on. With the kit's skills in every repo, an agent needs a test it can apply itself, or it either wraps a one-line fix in an issue and an approval or builds something large with neither.

**Moving only the review note into the PR was considered first, and rejected as too small.** It removes the bookkeeping and leaves the up-front task plan, the delta specs and the archive, which are the larger costs.

**A short brief file landed by its own PR was rejected.** It keeps a reviewed diff for the plan, a merge as the record of approval, and a copy in every clone. But the one plan PR measured drew no review, each correction to the plan becomes a commit in a stage's diff again, and the file has to be removed when the change ends.

**Keeping the build notes in a file deleted by the final stage was rejected.** It is easier to edit and to search, and a file can be collapsed in a PR's diff. It still costs a commit each time it changes, which is the note-keeping this removes, and it is agent-only text in the repository's history.

**Editing one build-notes comment in place was rejected.** It keeps the issue's thread shortest, but an edit replaces the whole comment, so one bad write loses the change's state, and how the notes stood at each gate survives only in GitHub's edit history, which nobody reads. **Appending only what each stage changed was rejected too:** the next agent would have to read every comment and work out which lines still hold, which is the accumulated note this ADR removes. A whole copy per gate costs a longer thread and gives both a single place to read and a history.

**Leaving the brief's history to GitHub's edit history was rejected**, for the reason given for the notes: it records that the text changed, not why, and nobody opens it. **Never editing the brief was rejected too:** a plan corrected only in comments beneath it is acted on, uncorrected, by whoever reads the body alone. So the body is edited and each edit is announced, and a stage's issue follows the same rule.

**A checklist of stages in the issue body was rejected for sub-issues.** A sub-issue gives the stage's steps somewhere to be written when the stage starts, gives a later stage somewhere to receive what an earlier one found, and is closed by its PR without anyone ticking a box. **A draft PR opened when the stage starts was rejected** as that home: it cannot exist before the stage has a commit, so it cannot receive what earlier stages find, and 041 retired the draft state for PRs that are open before they are ready to be read.

**Marking every stage blocked by the one before it was rejected.** It needs no judgement, but a chain says only what the order already says, and it has to be re-linked whenever a stage is split or moved - one change here went from seven stages to ten. Marking only real needs records something the order does not: which later stages a reviewer can reorder or drop between gates, and which stage in one change is waiting on a stage in another, as one here did.

**Keeping "look closely at" was considered.** It did once name the thing a gate was then sent back on. But it is the building agent reporting on itself to a reader who does not open it; a fresh review covers the same ground independently when it is asked for, and the choices the reviewer could overturn are asked as choices. The plan's equivalent, where the plan is most likely wrong, stays: it is one line, and it is the only pointer into a plan whose detail the reviewer does not read.

**Posting review findings inline on the PR was rejected.** It leaves the reviewer's page covered in findings already fixed. One comment saying what was found and what became of it keeps the record.

**Building the reviewers and the questions into the kit, with no way to swap them, was rejected,** and so was calling one named plugin's skills. A reviewer may already have a review skill they trust, and more than one exists; a kit that calls a particular plugin breaks in a repo without it. Roles with a default that a repo can fill keep the kit working alone and let it use what is installed.

**Using Matt Pocock's spec skill to write the plan, in place of a kit skill, was rejected.** Its spec is a different document from the brief: a long list of user stories where this has a sentence per requirement, implementation and testing decisions on the page the reviewer approves from, no place for the decisions that are the reviewer's or for where the plan is most likely wrong, and no approval before the work is marked ready. Those are the parts of the brief the evidence here asked for. That skill and the one that writes its tickets can also only be typed by a person, so a kit skill could not call them. The contract is what lets them be used anyway.

**Allowing one stage of a change in flight at a time was considered** as the guard against two sessions writing over each other. Claiming a stage by assigning it does the same without ruling out stages that could be built side by side.

**Keeping the full task plan as the default was rejected.** [specs.md](../../conventions/specs.md) argues that pricing every part of a change is how to find out whether to do it, and that a plan still growing is a reason to stop. That holds for a decision that is large and hard to undo, and it stays available for one: the steps of every stage are written into the first notes before approval, and a plan turned down keeps them on its closed issue. As the default it bought plans that were rewritten before they were built.

## Consequences

- **The history of a change is on GitHub, not in git.** A clone no longer carries it, a search of the repository no longer finds it, and it can be lost to a deleted issue or a move to another host. Looking back over past changes - the work this ADR is itself the result of - reads issues and pull requests through `gh` rather than files: `gh issue list --label change --state all --limit 200` finds them, and each one's stage PRs hold what departed, what review found and what was decided.
- **That lookback depends on the gate being written down.** The build notes say how the change stood at each gate, not what happened at it; that record is the stage PRs and their comments. A gate conversation held only in a terminal leaves nothing behind, and recording its outcome on the PR is what replaces the note that used to capture it. Nothing enforces it.
- **The plan is no longer a reviewed diff.** It is approved from an issue, and edits to an issue are not reviewed either. The approval comment and the comment paired with each edit are the whole record.
- **Text nobody has read is published as it is written.** The notes and the reviewer agents' findings go up under the reviewer's account with no diff in between, and each comment notifies whoever watches the repository. In a public repository the rule against naming a private one has to hold for that text too.
- **On a public repository anyone can comment on a change.** A file reached an agent only through a merged PR; an issue's thread does not. The rule that an agent follows only people who can write to the repository is what stands between a stranger's comment and a build, and the skill has to apply it when it reads a thread.
- **A workflow that needs GitHub issues and `gh`.** A repository hosted elsewhere, with issues turned off, or read during an outage has no plan to read. The kit already assumes `gh` for PRs; this makes it the only way to a change.
- **"Changed from the plan" means less than it did.** The approved plan is a line per stage, and the steps a stage is measured against are the agent's own, written when the stage starts.
- **No change is priced task by task before it starts** unless someone asks for that. The stage list is the estimate.
- **Nothing enforces a spec format.** That behaviour is written down at all now rests on tests and docs being kept, and on the final review's check that every requirement has a test.
- **More issues, and a longer thread.** A five-stage change is six issues and five PRs, and its issue carries one notes comment per gate, all but the last collapsed.
- **A review can be skipped.** The findings that argued for review came from passes nothing required, and nothing requires one now. A change can merge unreviewed by anything but its reviewer, and the only guard is that it says so.
- **A skill a repo names is not the kit's to vouch for.** Nothing checks that it does what its role needs; the kit's default covering what a named skill leaves out is the only guard, and a repo that names a weak reviewer gets a weaker gate.
- **The approval rules gain one line.** A change's stage issues are created without a further ask once its plan is approved, as its stage PRs already were. The rest falls under rules that exist: asking for a change to be planned is asking for its issue, posting and hiding the notes are comments, and the reviewer agents' "file it" findings wait for a yes at the gate, since filing an issue stays ask-first. So does closing a change.
- **The `pr` skill's body shape gives way for a stage PR**, whose body is the reviewer's page rather than a summary and a test plan.
- **The two skills are linked into every repo**, where OpenSpec's were opted into repo by repo ([021](021-adopt-openspec-centralised-via-kit.md)) to keep their descriptions out of sessions that would never use them. Two descriptions now load everywhere, against the six that an opted-in repo carried, and a repo needs no setup to plan a change.
- **The kit keeps the schema, the vendored skills and their scripts for now.** Consumers reach them through symlinks, so removing them would break every change still in flight. They go in a later change, once no consumer has a review-gated change open: `package.json` and its lockfile, the install, enable and refresh scripts, `repo-skills/` with its six vendored skills, `repo-skills-overlay/` and its check, and `schemas/review-gated/`. Two kit skills and a rewritten `conventions/specs.md` replace them.
- **A consumer's in-flight changes finish under the schema they started with.** Its `openspec/` directory, lint exclusions and skill symlinks are removed once the last one is archived; the living specs go with them, after anything the docs lack is moved across.
